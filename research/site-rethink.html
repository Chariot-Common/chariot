03 · Principles

What we take from it, without copying it

The work is the hero

No tagline above the fold competing with the projects. The studio's voice shows up in how things move and in the Studio page.

Reward attention, don't demand it

Every interaction should be missable on a first scroll. If someone notices it in a meeting and points it out, it's working.

Every project moves its own way

Each project gets a hover made for it: a deck assembling, an app scrolling, a logo drawing itself. The code stays one component and the clip does the talking.

Pieces before case studies

Any project can go live as a few strong pieces. A write-up is added when we have one, not required to publish.

Thinking sits next to the work

Philosophy, team and process move out of Careers into a Studio page that is reachable from anywhere.

Everything moves a little

Images focus in, edges soften, the dock floats, the mark rolls. Each motion is small and shares one set of timings, so together they feel like a single system.

06 · Updating

Adding a project without Framer

The goal is that adding a project means dropping a folder into the repo. Each project is one text file plus its media. The Index is generated from the pieces listed in those files, so it never needs updating by hand.

# content/projects/the-ken/index.md
title: The Ken
year: 2023
color: "#B8472E"                # tints the project sheet and its halftone
disciplines: [product, visual]
summary: Redesign of India's foremost business publication
poster: poster.avif             # still shown at rest
hover: hover.webm              # 2–4s clip, unique to this project
icon: icon.png                # tiny cut-out for the dock
pieces:                       # these feed the hover stack and the Index
  - { file: home.avif,    kind: product }
  - { file: paywall.avif, kind: product }
  - { file: wordmark.svg, kind: brand }
live: https://the-ken.com
featured: true                # show on the homepage
---
## The brief       2–3 sentences
## What we did     2–3 sentences
## What changed    2–3 sentences, a number if we have one

That fixed three-part write-up is roughly 150–250 words. It's short enough to write in one sitting, and a project can go live with an empty body and just its pieces.

Recommended

Files in the repo

Astro content collections (or Next with a typed loader). Schema checks every project at build. Vercel preview link for review before merge. No extra service or bill.

Later, if needed

Git-backed editor

Keystatic or TinaCMS on top of the same files, giving a form-based editor for anyone who doesn't want to touch the repo. Still no database.

Probably overkill

Headless CMS

Sanity, as dmcg uses. Worth it with many non-technical editors. Adds a vendor, a second place where content lives, and cost.

  • The reusable pattern. An /add-project command for Claude Code: point it at a folder of exports and a few lines of notes. It compresses images to AVIF, converts video to WebM and MP4, drafts the three-part write-up, fills in the file and opens a pull request with a preview link.
  • Media. Images can live in the repo and get optimised at build. Videos should go to object storage (Cloudflare R2 or Vercel Blob) so the repo stays small.
  • Asset kit per project. A poster still, a 2–4 second square hover clip (1080×1080, follow the recipe for its kind of work), a transparent icon for the dock, 4–8 pieces and notes for the write-up. Designers export this at handoff, while the files are still open.

08 · Art direction

Classical, slightly from the future

The work stays clean and quiet, as on dmcg. Chariot's personality lives around it: in the margins, the navigation and the small print. The look of that personality comes from old painting and bronze, made digital with halftone dots and real 3D objects.

Contra Labs, read closely
  • A 19th-century style oil painting (an angel in satin) is cropped huge and bleeds off the edge of the screen. She is typing on a laptop.
  • The laptop isn't painted. It's a flat black halftone dot pattern. The anachronism is played completely straight, which is why it feels confident, not jokey.
  • Halftone fields dissolve the painting into the page, and one section into the next. That dot layer is what makes it feel digital and a little futuristic.
  • Section cards are tight crops of museum objects used as metaphors: gilded helmets for "Arena", brass scales for "Benchmarking".
  • Light editorial serif headlines, small sans for UI, cream and muted teal.

It's Contra's look now, so we take the method, not the palette. Our subject is the chariot: Greek bronze instead of angels, cream and ink instead of teal, and our orange in the dots.

Four materials

PaintClassical and late-medieval oil painting for every figure: satin, gilded armour, soft light, serious faces.
BronzeReal 3D objects: a chariot wheel, the Charioteer of Delphi, a wax seal, a scroll. Turned and lit by the visitor's scroll and cursor.
DotsHalftone in ink and orange is the digital layer. Modern tools in a painting are drawn as dots, 3D objects render as dots, and images develop from dots into full colour.
ParchmentCream ground and a light editorial serif. Edges dissolve into dither gradients instead of ending at a line.

Building the 3D

  • One shader for everything. Three.js (react-three-fiber if we use React) with a single halftone shader shared by every object, so the wheel, the statue and the scroll look like one family.
  • Where models come from. Public-domain museum scans (Scan the World, Smithsonian Open Access, checking each licence), modelled in Blender, or image-to-3D tools like Meshy or Tripo for quick drafts.
  • Budget. One 3D object per screen, glTF with Draco compression under about 2MB, loaded only when it's about to be seen.
  • Fallback. Phones and reduced-motion settings get a pre-rendered video or still of the same object, in the same halftone.
  • Restraint. Objects only move in response to the visitor. Nothing spins on its own.
Weird Medieval Guys

Olivia Swarthout's account and book take real manuscript marginalia (the doodles monks drew around the text) and add deadpan modern captions. Figures are flat, expressionless and drawn without perspective, often hybrid creatures doing absurd things in the page border: a knight fighting a snail, a rabbit with a sword. Colour is ink, vermilion, lapis blue and gold on vellum. The joke depends on the art being real and carefully sourced.

What we take

  • Keep the medieval look exactly (painted, flat, calm faces) and swap the swords, spears and helmets for design tools: pen-tool nibs, rulers, cursors, colour wheels. Scenes are below.
  • Our team is already called the Charioteers, so each person can appear as one of these figures.
  • These drolleries live in the margins: beside the dock, in the footer, at the end of a write-up, on the 404 page. Never on top of client work.
  • Each has a one-line deadpan caption that appears on hover.
teenage engineering

The product is shot as a lone object on grey or black, and scroll drives it: the OP-1 field physically stretches longer as you move down the page ("the beauty of evolution"). The nav is a strip of hairline drawings, each with a lowercase label and three tiny sublinks under it. The copy argues with the reader ("hold on… this shadow is unrealistically long"). Specs are long lists of tiny uppercase text. Custom type throughout.

What we take

  • One line weight for every icon and diagram. That's what makes their site feel like one instrument. The painted figures are the only exception.
  • Nav items with three tiny sublinks, so the whole site is visible at a glance (sketch below).
  • A small spec sheet on every project: year, team, weeks, platforms, disciplines. It's cheap to write and reads as confidence.
  • Humour in the microcopy only: footer, 404, empty states, sound toggle. The project write-ups stay straight.

Style prompt (added to every scene when you copy it)

Classical oil painting in the style of late-medieval and Renaissance masters, with satin drapery, gilded armour and soft directional light, calm and serious expressions. Every modern design tool in the scene (stylus, laptop, cursor, ruler, pen-tool nib) is rendered as a flat black halftone dot pattern like a screen print, while everything else stays painted. The painting is cropped and its edges dissolve into a halftone dither gradient fading into a warm cream background. Muted palette of cream, bronze, ink and deep teal, with small burnt-orange accents only in the halftone. Editorial, elegant, played completely straight. No 3D render look, no cartoon, no readable text except where named.

Use these to explore and agree on a direction. The final figures should be redrawn by hand, by us or an illustrator, so they look made rather than generated and stay consistent with each other. Square 1:1, and a version on plain vellum without the background text so it sits cleanly in a margin.

Guardrails, so it never tips into theme-park medieval

  • Final art made from the agreed scenes by us or an illustrator, then halftoned in code so every piece shares the same dots.
  • The painted palette stays inside the figures. The site around them stays in our own colours, with our orange living in the dots.
  • Take Contra's method, not its look: no angels, no teal sections, no laptop-in-a-painting hero.
  • At most one figure per screen, under 160px tall, always in a margin.
  • If someone scrolls past without noticing any of it, the site should still feel finished.
09 · Sound

Rules for sound so it stays subtle

  • Off until the visitor turns it on with the corner toggle. Browsers block audio before an interaction anyway, and surprise sound in an office is the fastest way to get a tab closed.
  • Two sounds at most: a soft tick on hovering a project and a slightly lower one on opening it. Each under 80ms, generated with Web Audio so there's nothing to download.
  • No sound on scroll, and none on touch devices.
  • Remember the visitor's choice. Respect reduced-motion settings by also keeping sound off.
